\section*{Pissarides’ analysis of taxation and variable search intensity}
\paragraph{a.} According to the setting, the timing protocol of the model is:
at the beginning of the period, the worker chooses the level of effort, 
then she instantaneously observes the probability of receiving an offer.
If the offer comes, she also needs to decide whether to accept the offer at the beginning of this period.
Therefore, the worker's problem can be characterized by the following value function.
\begin{align*}
    U = \max_{e} \left( 1 - \pi(e) \right) (1 - e + z + \beta U) + 
    \pi (e) \int_{0}^B \max_{\{\text{Accept, Reject}\}} \bigg\{& w - e - T(w) + \beta (\alpha U + (1 - \alpha) (w - T(w))), \\
    & 1 - e + z + \beta U \bigg\} dF(w)
\end{align*}
where $U$ denotes the value of unemployment.
%
%

The optimal strategy is given by the independent choices of optimal level and a reservation wages. 
In particular, 
at the reservation wage $\bar{w}$,
the indifference condition of accepting/rejecting an offer is 
\[
\bar{w} - e - T(\bar{w}) + \beta \bigg[\alpha U + (1 - \alpha)\left[\bar{w} - T(\bar{w}) \right] \bigg] = 1 - e + z + \beta U
\]
Rearranging terms, we have 
\[
\left[1 + \beta  (1 - \alpha)\right] \left[\bar{w} - T(\bar{w}) \right]  = 1 + z + \beta(1- \alpha) U
\]
Since $U$ is a constant, we can conclude that $\bar{w}$ does NOT depend on the optimal effort.
\\
\textbf{Remark:} Intuitively, the choice of $e$ is a \textbf{sunk cost}, which won't affect the subsequent decisions.
%

To characterize the optimal choice of $e$, I first simplify the equation of $U$:
\begin{align*}
    U = \max_{e} 1 - e + z + \beta U - \pi(e) (1 + z + \beta U) + 
    \pi (e) \int_{0}^B \max_{\{\text{Accept, Reject}\}} \bigg\{& w - T(w) + \beta (\alpha U + (1 - \alpha) (w - T(w))), \\
    & 1 + z + \beta U \bigg\} dF(w)
\end{align*}

Next, I denote the objective function by $g(e)$, and denote 
$\int_{0}^B \max_{\{\text{Accept, Reject}\}} \bigg\{ w - T(w) + \beta (\alpha U + (1 - \alpha) (w - T(w))), 1 + z + \beta U \bigg\} dF(w)$ by $\Omega$.
I compute the derivative of $g(e)$ w.r.t. $e$ and obtain 
\[
\pi'(e) = \frac{1}{\Omega - (1 + z + \beta U)}
\]


\paragraph{b.}
Assume that $T(w) = t(w -a)$, then the indifference condition becomes 
\[
\left[1 + \beta  (1 - \alpha)\right] \left[(1-t) \bar{w} + ta \right]  = 1 + z + \beta(1- \alpha) U
\].
It follows that 
\[
\bar{w} = - \frac{t}{1 - t} a + \frac{1 + z + \beta (1 - \alpha )U}{1 + \beta (1 - \alpha) (1 - t)}
\].
Thus, we have $\frac{\partial \bar{w}}{\partial a} = - \frac{t}{1 - t} < 0$.

To examine the effect of increasing $a$ on the effort, 
I consider the function $h(e) = \pi'(e) - \frac{1}{\Omega - (1 + z + \beta U)}$.
Also note that $\frac{\partial \Omega}{\partial a} = \left( 1 - F(\bar{w})\right)(1 + \beta (1 - \alpha)t) > 0$.
Applying the implicit differenetial equation, I have 
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial e^*}{\partial a} & = -\frac{\partial h/ \partial a}{\partial h/ \partial e^*} \\ 
& = - \frac{\frac{\frac{\partial \Omega}{\partial a}}{(\Omega - (1 + z + \beta U))^2}}{\pi''(e)} > 0
\end{align*}
This concludes that increasing $a$ increases the optimal level of search effort.
As the distribution of wage $F(w)$ is fixed, and the reservation wage decreases,
it immediately follows that the probability of accepting a job increases.

\paragraph{c.} 
The derivative of $\bar{w}$ with respect to $t$ is 
\[
\frac{\partial \bar{w}}{\partial t} = - \frac{a}{(1 - t)^2},
\]
which is positive if $a < 0$.

The derivative of $e^*$ with respect to $t$ is 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial e^*}{\partial t} & = -\frac{\partial h/ \partial t}{\partial h/ \partial e^*} \\  
    & = - \frac{\beta (1 - \alpha)(a - \mathbb{E}_+[w]) + a - F(\bar{w}) [a + \beta a (1 - \alpha)] }{\pi''(e)}
\end{align*}
If the derivative is negative, we need 
\[
\frac{\beta (1 - \alpha)(a - \mathbb{E}_+[w]) + a - F(\bar{w}) [a + \beta a (1 - \alpha)] }{\pi''(e)} > 0
\]
That is, we need 
\[
\beta (1 - a) (a - \mathbb{E}_+[w]) + a < F(\bar{w})[a + \beta a (1 - \alpha)]
\]



\section*{2. Convergence results and contraction mapping}
\paragraph{a.} To show that the limit is unique, one can apply the Triangle Inequality. 

Concretely, for any positive $\epsilon$, there exists $N$, such that 
\[
\rho(x, y) \leq \rho(x_n, x) + \rho(x_n, y) < \frac{\epsilon}{2} + \frac{\epsilon}{2} = \epsilon
\]
Since $\epsilon$ can be arbitrary small, we conclude that $\rho(x, y) \rightarrow 0$.
Therefore, the limit is unique. \(\blacksquare \)

\paragraph{b.} To show that convergence implies Cauchy, one can apply the Triangle Inequality. 
Concretely, denote the limit by $x$, for any $m, k \in \mathbb{N}$, and any positive $\epsilon$, 
there exists an integer $N$, s.t.
\[
\rho(x_m, x_k) \leq \rho(x_m, x) + \rho(x_k, x) < \frac{\epsilon}{2} + \frac{\epsilon}{2} = \epsilon
\]
for any $m, k > N$.
Therefore, convergence implies Cauchy. \(\blacksquare \)


\paragraph{c.} Note that if $(x_n)$ is a Cauchy sequence,
then for any positive $\epsilon$, there exists $N$, such that 
$\rho(x_m, x_k) < \epsilon$ for any $m, k \geq N$.
Now take $k = N$,
we have $\rho(x_m, x_N) < \epsilon$ for any $m > N$.
By the inverse Triangle Inequality, we have 
\[
\rho(x_m, 0) < \rho(x_N, 0) + \epsilon
\]
Therefore, we bound the sequence by 
\[
\max\{ \max\{|x_1|, \ldots, |x_{N-1}|\}, |x_N| + \epsilon\}
\]

\paragraph{d.} We can prove the counter-positive statement, i.e., 
\[
\exists \text{ a subseq of } x_n \text{ does not converge to } x \Leftrightarrow x_n \text{does not converge to } x
\]
\paragraph{Proof}
``$\Rightarrow$''
Let $(x_{m_k})$ be a subsequence that does not converge to $x$.
This implies that, there exists a positive $\epsilon$, for any $M \in \mathbb{N}$, there exist some $m_k > M$, 
such that $|x_{m_k} - x| > \epsilon $. 
Since $m \rightarrow \infty$ as $m_k \rightarrow \infty$, we conclude that $x_n$ does not converge to $x$ as well. 

``$\Leftarrow$''
Let $x_n$ be a sequence that does not converge to $x$.
For a given $\epsion > 0$,
there exists infinitely many $x_n$ such that $|x_n - x| > \epsilon$.
Therefore, one can construct a subsequence $(X_{m_k})$ by select elements from the set $\{x: |x_n - x| > \epsilon \}$.
\(\blacksquare\)

\section*{3. Linear contraction mapping}
\paragraph{a.} ``Monotonicity'' requires that for $x1 > x2$, $g(x1) > g(x2)$,
which implies $b > 0$.
``Discounting'' requires that $\exists \beta \in (0, 1)$, such that,
$g(x+c) \leq g(x) + \beta c$, that is, 
\begin{align*}
& a + bx + bc \leq a + bx + \beta c \\
\Rightarrow & b \leq \beta
\end{align*}
In summary, $g(x)$ requires $b \in (0,1)$ to be a Contraction Mapping. 

\paragraph{b.} Since $g(x)$ will intersect $f(x) = x$ only once for any values of $a$ and $b$, 
we don't need to restrict $a$ and $b$ to guarantee the existance and uniqueness of the fixed point. 
This answer differs in that in \textbf{a.} because contraction is not a necessary and sufficient condition of getting a unique fixed point. 

\paragraph{c.} 
If $\mathcal{S} = [0, 1]$, the condition for a unqiue fixed point is given by 
\[
\frac{a}{1 - b} \in [0, 1]
\]
To show that the condition in \textbf{a.} won't guarantee a unique fixed piont if $\mathcal{S} = [0, 1]$,
we can consider cases where the fixed point is outside $[0, 1]$.
